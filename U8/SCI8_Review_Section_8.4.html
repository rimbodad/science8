Section Roadmap

What this lesson is about, and why it matters

What this section is about: In Topic 8.3 you learned how metals react with oxygen. In this section, you will explore what happens when metals react with water instead. Some metals react violently, some react slowly, some only react with steam, and some do not react at all.

Why it matters: Understanding how reactive a metal is helps scientists and engineers decide which metals are safe to use for pipes, roofs, coins, and jewellery. It also helps you understand why sodium and potassium must be stored very carefully.

By the end of this lesson, I can...

  • describe how different metals react with cold water, hot water and steam
  • write word equations for metal + water reactions
  • use evidence to put metals in order of reactivity
  • explain, using particle theory, why steam reacts faster than cold water
  • explain why some metals (like gold) barely react at all

Core Concepts

Understand the science, one idea at a time

1. Metals react with water to make a hydroxide and hydrogen

Main idea: When a reactive metal meets water, a chemical reaction happens. Two new substances are formed: a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

Word equation: metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

Example: sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen

Remember: hydrogen gas is always one of the products when a metal reacts with water.

2. The reactivity series ranks metals from most to least reactive

Main idea: Not all metals react with water in the same way. Scientists arrange metals in a list called the reactivity series, based on evidence from experiments.

How we build it: We observe how fast a metal reacts, how many bubbles are made, and whether the water gets hot or catches fire. Metals that react fastest and most strongly are placed near the top.

Remember: reactivity is judged from evidence — speed of reaction, bubbling, heat, or flame — not by guessing.

3. Sodium and potassium react violently with cold water

Main idea: Sodium and potassium are so reactive that they react immediately with cold water and even with water vapour in the air.

Explanation: This is why they must be stored under oil — the oil keeps water and air away from the metal surface.

Evidence: Sodium moves and fizzes on the surface of the water. Potassium reacts even more strongly — so much thermal energy is released that the hydrogen gas catches fire.

Common misconception: Some students think the metal itself is burning. In fact, it is the hydrogen gas produced by the reaction that catches fire.

4. Calcium and magnesium react more gently with cold water

Main idea: Calcium reacts with cold water, but much less violently than sodium or potassium. Magnesium reacts even more slowly, and the reaction can be hard to see.

Example: calcium + water → calcium hydroxide + hydrogen

Remember: a slower bubbling reaction still shows a chemical change is happening — you may need to wait, or use hot water.

5. Some metals only react with steam, not cold water

Main idea: Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water, but reacts much more quickly with steam.

Word equation: magnesium + water (g) → magnesium oxide + hydrogen

Why, using particle theory: Steam particles have much more energy than cold water particles. They move faster and collide with the metal particles more often and with more force, so the reaction happens faster.

Note: "water (g)" means water in the gas state — steam.

6. Some metals barely react with water at all

Main idea: Metals such as copper, silver and gold are very unreactive. Gold does not react with water or oxygen at all, and is described as inert.

Evidence: Gold stays shiny even after being buried in soil for hundreds of years. Silver slowly reacts with substances in the air over a long time, but not with water.

Remember: "does not react with water" is not always the same as "completely inert" — inert metals do not react with almost anything.

Diagram Mastery

See the science — diagrams help you remember the ideas

Diagram 1 — The Reactivity Series (metals with water)

Caption: Metals near the top react with water quickly and strongly. Metals near the bottom barely react at all.

What to notice: potassium and sodium react even with cold water; zinc and copper only react with steam or barely react; gold does not react with water.
Common mistake: writing metals in a random order. Always use evidence (speed of bubbling, heat, flame) to decide the order.

Diagram 2 — Investigating calcium and water

Caption: The funnel traps gas released by the reaction. The gas pushes water out of the test tube, so the gas can be collected and tested.

What to notice: the gas collects by pushing water out of the test tube — this method is called collection by displacement of water.
Common mistake: forgetting that the gas collected is hydrogen, not air. You can confirm this using the lighted-splint test.

Diagram 3 — Magnesium reacting with steam

Caption: Heat turns the water in the ceramic wool into steam. The steam reacts with the hot magnesium ribbon, producing magnesium oxide and hydrogen gas.

What to notice: the hydrogen gas produced can be lit and burns at the open end of the tube.
Common mistake: thinking the magnesium is reacting with water vapour in the air. It is actually reacting with steam made from the ceramic wool.

Scientific Thinking

Think like a scientist — use evidence to reason

1. Interpreting evidence — ranking reactivity

Jamila tests four substances with cold water and records her observations.

MetalObservation
Calciumsinks; bubbles slowly; forms a colourless solution
Potassiummoves rapidly on the surface; bubbles violently; lilac flame seen
Silversinks; no bubbles seen
Metal Xmoves around the surface; bubbles rapidly; forms a colourless solution

Think: Using only the evidence in the table, which metals are clearly less reactive than Metal X? Which is clearly more reactive? Explain your reasoning using the observations, not just your memory of the reactivity series.

2. Comparing — cold water vs steam

Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water, but reacts much faster with steam.

Think: Describe one similarity and one difference between the two reactions (the products, and the speed).

3. Explaining cause and effect — particle theory

Think: Use particle theory to explain why steam reacts with magnesium faster than cold water does. Try to use the words "energy", "move" and "collide" in your explanation.

4. Evaluating an explanation — what does "inert" really mean?

A classmate says: "Copper is inert because it does not react with water."

Think: Is this statement fully correct? What is missing? (Hint: think about what "inert" means, and whether not reacting with water is enough evidence.)

Real World Science

Where does this science appear in everyday life?

Copper pipes and roofs

Real world situation: Copper is used for water pipes and for roofing on some buildings.

Scientific connection: Copper does not react with water, so it does not corrode when it is constantly wet.

Why it matters: A metal used for pipes or roofs must not react with rain or running water, or the structure would be damaged over time.

Why magnesium is not used for roofs

Real world situation: Magnesium is a useful, lightweight metal, but it is never used for roofing.

Scientific connection: Magnesium reacts slowly with water, so rain or snow would gradually react with a magnesium roof.

Why it matters: Choosing the right metal for a job depends on its reactivity, not only on its weight or cost.

Why sodium and potassium are stored under oil

Real world situation: In science laboratories, sodium and potassium are always kept under oil.

Scientific connection: These metals react so strongly with water — even water vapour in the air — that the oil layer is needed to keep them safe and unreacted.

Why it matters: This shows how reactivity affects how a substance must be stored and handled safely.

Workbook Preview

Get ready for Exercise 8.4 in your Workbook — no answers shown here

Ranking metals in order of reactivity

Learning objective: Use experimental results to order metals from most to least reactive.

Hint 1: Look for clues like speed of bubbling, movement of the metal, and whether a flame appears.
Hint 2: The metal with the fastest, strongest reaction goes at the top of your list.

Thinking strategy: Write down the evidence first, then decide the order — do not guess from memory alone.

Writing word equations

Learning objective: Write a word equation for a metal reacting with water or steam.

Hint 1: Remember the pattern: metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen.
Hint 2: If the question says "steam", write "water (g)" instead of "water" and the product will be a metal oxide, not a hydroxide.

Labelling the steam apparatus diagram

Learning objective: Identify and label the parts of the apparatus used to react a metal with steam.

Hint: Think about where the heat is applied, where the water/steam is produced, and where the metal ribbon sits.

Testing for hydrogen gas

Learning objective: Describe how to test for hydrogen gas.

Hint: Think about what tool you would use, and what sound you would listen for.

Full-Mark Tip

Word equation questions usually award marks for each correctly named substance and for the correct order (reactants before the arrow, products after). Take care with spelling of "hydroxide" and "hydrogen" — they look similar but are different.
