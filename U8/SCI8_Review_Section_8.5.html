Lesson Roadmap

What this section is about: You will learn what happens when metals react with dilute acids, how to describe and observe these reactions, and how to compare how reactive different metals are.

Why it matters: Metal and acid reactions happen in laboratories, industry and even inside batteries. Understanding reactivity also helps you predict how metals behave and why some metals are far too dangerous to test with acid.

By the end of this lesson, I can...

  • Explain what happens when a metal reacts with a dilute acid.
  • Write a word equation for a metal reacting with an acid.
  • Describe how to observe and measure a reaction between a metal and an acid.
  • Compare the reactivity of different metals with dilute acids.
  • Explain why some metals must never be tested with acid in the school laboratory.
  • Plan a fair test to compare the reactivity of metals.

Core Concepts

Read each idea one at a time. Try to explain it in your own words before moving to the next card.

1. Metals React With Acids to Form a Salt and Hydrogen

Main idea: When a reactive metal is added to a dilute acid, the metal reacts to form a new substance called a salt, and a gas called hydrogen is released.

This always follows the same general pattern, no matter which metal or acid is used:

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen

Example: magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

Common misconception: The metal does not simply "disappear". Its atoms are not destroyed — they become part of the new salt, which usually dissolves in the liquid.

2. The Acid Used Decides the Name of the Salt

Main idea: The type of acid decides the ending of the salt's name.

hydrochloric acid → a chloride  |  sulfuric acid → a sulfate  |  nitric acid → a nitrate

Example: zinc + nitric acid → zinc nitrate + hydrogen

The metal's name usually stays the same in the salt — only the acid decides the ending.

3. How to Tell a Reaction Is Happening

Main idea: You can observe a reaction between a metal and an acid in several ways.

Look for: bubbles of gas forming, the metal getting smaller, and a rise in temperature.

Because heat energy is released, this reaction is described as exothermic.

Bubbles + rising temperature = a chemical reaction is happening.

4. Metals React at Different Speeds — Reactivity

Main idea: Different metals react with the same dilute acid at very different speeds.

A very reactive metal, such as magnesium, produces bubbles quickly and vigorously. A less reactive metal, such as iron, reacts more slowly. Some metals, such as copper and gold, do not react with dilute acid at all — they are described as inert (unreactive).

Common misconception: Seeing more bubbles in a set time tells you the reaction is faster — it does not automatically mean more salt has been produced overall.

5. Some Metals Are Too Dangerous to Test With Acid

Main idea: Potassium, sodium and calcium react so vigorously that adding them to dilute acid in a school laboratory would be extremely dangerous.

These metals could react explosively, so they must never be used in this investigation.

Potassium, sodium and calcium — too reactive and dangerous to test with acid in class.

6. Testing for Hydrogen Gas

Main idea: Scientists confirm that a gas is hydrogen using the "squeaky pop" test.

A lighted splint is held near the mouth of the test tube. If the gas is hydrogen, it burns rapidly and makes a squeaky pop sound.

7. Planning a Fair Test to Compare Reactivity

Main idea: To compare metals fairly, only the type of metal should change.

Keep the same: type of acid, concentration of acid, volume of acid, and the mass or size of each piece of metal.

Measure reactivity by comparing the number of bubbles produced in a set time, or by timing how long it takes to collect a set volume of hydrogen gas.

Change only ONE variable — the metal. Keep everything else the same.

Diagram 1: The General Reaction Pattern

What it shows: Every metal–acid reaction follows this same pattern of reactants (left) forming products (right).

Notice: The arrow shows a change happening — a chemical reaction, not just mixing.

Common mistake: Writing the arrow backwards or forgetting one of the two products.

Diagram 2: Comparing Reactivity in Test Tubes

What it shows: Four metals placed in equal volumes of the same dilute acid.

Notice: Metal A produces the most bubbles quickly, while Metal D produces none — Metal A is the most reactive and Metal D does not react.

Common mistake: Forgetting that this comparison is only fair if the acid, its volume, and the size of each metal piece are the same.

Diagram 3: Collecting and Measuring Hydrogen Gas

What it shows: Hydrogen gas travels along the delivery tube and is collected by displacing water in an upside-down test tube.

Notice: The volume of gas collected can be timed to compare how quickly different metals react.

Common mistake: Starting the timer before the delivery tube is properly back in place, which gives an inaccurate time.

Diagram 4: Reactivity Order

What it shows: A general order of reactivity for common metals with dilute acid, based on classroom investigations.

Notice: The order matches the amount of bubbling and speed of reaction observed in Diagram 2.

Common mistake: Assuming every metal reacts — copper and gold do not react with dilute acid at all.

Scientific Thinking

Think like a scientist. Write your own answer before checking the guidance.

Interpret the Evidence

Look again at Diagram 2. Which metal, A or D, is more reactive? Explain how you know using the diagram.

Guidance: Metal A produces many bubbles quickly, showing it reacts fast — it is more reactive. Metal D produces no bubbles, so it does not react with the acid.

Identify an Unfair Test

Two students compare metal reactivity. One uses 20 cm³ of dilute acid, and the other uses 30 cm³ of the same acid. Is this a fair test? Explain your reasoning.

Guidance: No, it is not fair. The volume of acid must be a control variable — using different volumes could make one reaction appear faster even if the metals are equally reactive.

Make a Prediction

Using Diagram 4, predict what you would observe if you placed a piece of iron and a piece of zinc into separate test tubes of the same dilute acid.

Guidance: The zinc should produce bubbles more quickly than the iron, because zinc is more reactive than iron.

Evaluate a Statement

A student says: "If we see more bubbles in the first ten seconds, that means more salt has been made overall." Do you agree? Explain why.

Guidance: Not necessarily. More bubbles in a short time shows a faster reaction rate, but it does not automatically mean more salt was produced in total — that depends on how much metal and acid react completely.

Real World Science

Where can I see this science in real life?

Why Gold and Silver Jewellery Stay Shiny

Situation: Gold and silver jewellery can be worn every day, even though skin produces slightly acidic sweat.

Scientific connection: Gold and silver are very unreactive metals, so they do not easily react with weak acids.

Why it matters: This is one reason unreactive metals are chosen for jewellery — they resist chemical change over long periods of time.

Acid Rain and Metal Structures

Situation: Rain can be slightly acidic, especially near cities with air pollution.

Scientific connection: Over many years, acidic rainwater can slowly react with reactive metals on the surface of buildings, bridges or statues.

Why it matters: Engineers often choose unreactive metals, or coat reactive metals, to protect structures from this kind of slow chemical damage.

Testing for Hydrogen in the Lab and Industry

Situation: Scientists sometimes need to confirm that a gas produced in a reaction is hydrogen.

Scientific connection: The "squeaky pop" test with a lighted splint is a simple version of tests used to identify hydrogen gas safely.

Why it matters: Hydrogen gas is increasingly used as a clean fuel, so being able to identify and handle it safely is an important scientific skill.

Command Word

"Describe" vs "Explain": "Describe" asks for observations or facts (e.g. what you see). "Explain" asks you to give reasons (e.g. why it happens).

High Priority Skill

Common Trap

When describing a fair test, students often forget to mention both the volume and the concentration of acid used. Full marks usually need both control variables stated.
