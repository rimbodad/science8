What is this section about?

Some chemical reactions take in thermal energy instead of giving it out. These are called endothermic reactions. In this section you will learn how to recognise them, why the temperature drops, and how this idea is used in everyday life — like sherbet sweets and cold packs.

Why it matters: Once you understand energy changes in reactions, you can explain many things you notice every day — a cool feeling in your mouth, a cold pack for an injury, or why ice takes time to melt.

Your learning objectives — by the end of this section you should be able to:

  • Explain what an endothermic reaction is, using the idea of energy being absorbed.
  • Tell the difference between an endothermic reaction and an endothermic process.
  • Use particle theory to explain what happens when ice melts.
  • Interpret temperature data to decide whether a change is endothermic or exothermic.
  • Describe real-life uses of endothermic reactions and processes.

A. Core Science Vocabulary
endothermic reaction
/ˌen.dəʊˈθɜː.mɪk riˈæk.ʃən/ · en‑do‑ther‑mic re‑ac‑tion
Simple meaning: a chemical change that takes in heat.
Scientific meaning: a reaction in which thermal energy is absorbed from the surroundings and stored as chemical energy, so new substances form and the temperature falls.
Example: "Mixing citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate is an endothermic reaction."
Tip: "endo" means "into" — energy goes IN.
endothermic process
/ˌen.dəʊˈθɜː.mɪk ˈprəʊ.ses/ · en‑do‑ther‑mic pro‑cess
Simple meaning: a change that takes in heat, but does not make a new substance.
Scientific meaning: a physical change, such as a change of state, in which thermal energy is absorbed from the surroundings without any new substance forming.
Example: "Ice melting is an endothermic process, not a reaction."
Tip: process = state change only, no new substance.
exothermic
/ˌek.səʊˈθɜː.mɪk/ · ex‑o‑ther‑mic
Simple meaning: a change that gives out heat.
Scientific meaning: a reaction or process in which thermal energy is transferred to the surroundings, so the temperature rises.
Example: "Burning wood is exothermic — it warms the air around it."
Tip: "exo" means "out" — this is the opposite of endothermic.
thermal energy
/ˈθɜː.məl ˈen.ə.dʒi/ · ther‑mal en‑er‑gy
Simple meaning: heat energy.
Scientific meaning: the energy transferred between objects or substances because of a temperature difference.
Example: "Thermal energy moves from the hot reaction into the cool surroundings."
surroundings
/səˈraʊn.dɪŋz/ · sur‑round‑ings
Simple meaning: everything around a reaction.
Scientific meaning: the container, air, and surrounding materials that thermal energy can be transferred to or from.
Example: "In an endothermic reaction, energy is taken from the surroundings."
reactant / product
/riˈæk.tənt/ · /ˈprɒd.ʌkt/
Simple meaning: a reactant is a starting substance; a product is a new substance formed.
Example: "Citric acid and sodium hydrogencarbonate are reactants. Water and carbon dioxide are products."
dissolve
/dɪˈzɒlv/ · dis‑solve
Simple meaning: when a solid mixes completely into a liquid.
Scientific meaning: particles of a solute spread evenly through a solvent to form a solution; no new substance is formed.
Example: "Potassium chloride dissolves in water — this is an endothermic process."

Core Concepts

1. What is an endothermic reaction?

Main idea: An endothermic reaction absorbs thermal energy from its surroundings and stores it as chemical energy in new bonds.

Why it happens: Some reactions need more energy to break the bonds in the reactants than they release when new bonds form in the products. That extra energy is pulled in from the surroundings, so the surroundings feel cooler.

Evidence: If you mix citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate, the temperature drops. This proves energy is being taken in, not given out.

Example: sodium hydrogencarbonate + citric acid → sodium citrate + water + carbon dioxide.

⚠ Common misconception
Some students think a cooling feeling means "nothing is happening." In fact, a temperature drop is strong evidence that a chemical reaction is happening — just an endothermic one.

2. Endothermic reaction vs. endothermic process — what's the difference?

Main idea: Both take in thermal energy, but only a reaction makes new substances.

How to tell them apart: Ask yourself — has a new substance been formed?

  • Yes → it is an endothermic reaction (e.g. citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate).
  • No, it is only a state or physical change → it is an endothermic process (e.g. ice melting, potassium chloride dissolving in water).
⚠ Common misconception
Ice melting is often wrongly called a "reaction." No new substance is formed — solid water simply changes to liquid water — so it is a process, not a reaction.

3. Explaining ice melting using particle theory

Main idea: As ice absorbs thermal energy, its particles gain enough energy to break free from fixed positions and become liquid.

How it happens: In ice, particles vibrate in fixed positions because strong forces hold them together. As thermal energy is absorbed from the surroundings, the particles vibrate faster. Eventually they have enough energy to overcome the forces holding them in place, and they can move and slide past each other — the ice becomes liquid water.

Key sentence to remember: "Ice melting is an endothermic process because particles absorb thermal energy from the surroundings to overcome the forces between them."

4. Using endothermic and exothermic changes

Main idea: Scientists and engineers design useful products based on whether a change absorbs or releases energy.

Endothermic example — chemical ice packs: Two compartments (often ammonium nitrate and water) are separated inside a pack. When the pack is squeezed, the seal breaks, the substances mix, and the ammonium nitrate dissolves. This is an endothermic process, so the pack feels cold — useful for treating injuries without a freezer.

Exothermic contrast — self-heating cans: Calcium oxide and water are kept apart until the can is opened. When they mix, heat is given out, warming food or drink. This works well in remote places or emergencies.

Everyday example: Sherbet sweets contain dry citric acid and sodium hydrogencarbonate. When you suck them, they dissolve in your saliva and react — an endothermic reaction that gives a cool, refreshing feeling.

5. Deciding if a change is endothermic or exothermic from evidence

Main idea: Compare the starting and final temperature to decide.

  • Final temperature lower than the start → endothermic (energy absorbed).
  • Final temperature higher than the start → exothermic (energy released).

Example: If the temperature falls from 19 °C to 16 °C, the change is endothermic — energy has been taken in from the surroundings.

Diagram Mastery

Diagram 1 — Energy flow: exothermic vs. endothermic

What it shows: The direction of arrows shows which way thermal energy moves. Exothermic reactions send energy outward (temperature rises). Endothermic reactions pull energy inward (temperature falls).

What to notice: The reaction container is the same — only the direction of energy transfer is different.

Common mistake: Thinking "cold" means no reaction is happening. A drop in temperature is real evidence of an endothermic reaction.

Diagram 2 — Particle model: ice melting (an endothermic process)

What it shows: As ice absorbs thermal energy, particles vibrate more until they can move freely as a liquid.

What to notice: The particles themselves do not change — only their arrangement and movement change.

Common mistake: Believing new particles are created when ice melts. No new substance forms — this is why melting is a process, not a reaction.

Diagram 3 — Investigation setup: testing for an endothermic reaction

What it shows: How to measure temperature before and after adding sodium hydrogencarbonate to citric acid.

What to notice: The thermometer measures temperature only — never use it to stir, as this can break the bulb.

Common mistake: Forgetting to record the starting temperature before adding the solid — without it, you cannot calculate the temperature change.

Real World Science

Sherbet sweets

Situation: Sherbet contains dry citric acid and sodium hydrogencarbonate. Connection: When it dissolves in saliva, an endothermic reaction takes place. Why it matters: This explains the cool, fizzy feeling in your mouth.

Chemical ice packs for injuries

Situation: Sports injuries are often treated with instant cold packs. Connection: Breaking the pack allows ammonium nitrate to dissolve in water, an endothermic process. Why it matters: The pack cools quickly without needing a freezer.

Self-heating cans (the exothermic contrast)

Situation: Campers and emergency workers sometimes use self-heating food or drink cans. Connection: Calcium oxide reacting with water is exothermic, releasing heat. Why it matters: Comparing this with cold packs helps you see the two types of energy change side by side.

Exercise: Energy changes

Deciding endothermic or exothermic from a table

Learning objective: Use start and final temperatures to classify a change.

Hint 1: Subtract the start temperature from the final temperature. A negative result means energy was absorbed.

Hint 2: Check whether a new substance has formed before deciding "reaction" or "process."

Thinking tip: Always write your reasoning in a full sentence — for example, "the temperature fell, so energy was absorbed."

Try this yourself first before checking any answers with your teacher or parent.

Exercise: Exothermic or endothermic?

Classifying several reactions and processes

Learning objective: Compare several results and identify which shows the greatest energy change.

Hint 1: Look for the largest difference between start and final temperature.

Hint 2: Think about why a polystyrene cup gives a more accurate reading than a glass beaker.

Thinking tip: Insulators reduce energy loss or gain to the surroundings, which improves reliability.

Exercise: Endothermic reactions and processes

Explaining everyday cooling situations

Learning objective: Apply particle theory to explain cooling in everyday situations, such as evaporation.

Hint 1: Think about what happens to particles as a liquid changes to a gas.

Hint 2: Ask where the energy for the change comes from.

Thinking tip: Structure your explanation as: particles → energy → change of state → effect on surroundings.
