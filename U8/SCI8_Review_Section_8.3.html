What this section is about

Metals are all around us — in bridges, coins, cutlery, and buildings. But metals do not stay the same forever. Many of them slowly react with the oxygen in the air. This is called an oxidation reaction.

In this section, you will learn why some metals react with oxygen quickly, why some react very slowly, and why some barely react at all. You will also learn why iron rusts, and how people protect iron so it lasts longer.

Why it matters Understanding how metals react with oxygen helps engineers choose the right metal for bridges, ships, pipes, and tools — and helps you understand why old iron objects fall apart over time.

Learning objectives

  • I can describe how different metals react with oxygen.
  • I can compare how reactive different metals are, using evidence.
  • I can explain the conditions needed for iron to rust.
  • I can suggest ways to protect iron from rusting.

Core Concepts

1. Metals react with oxygen — this is called oxidation

Many metals react with oxygen if they get hot enough. This is an oxidation reaction, and it makes a new substance called a metal oxide.

The general word equation is always the same shape:

metal + oxygen → metal oxide

Not every metal reacts at the same speed. Magnesium reacts quickly and brightly. Iron reacts much more slowly. This difference in speed tells us how reactive a metal is.

2. Some metals are so reactive they react without being heated

Sodium, potassium and calcium are so reactive that they react with oxygen in the air at room temperature — no heating needed. When these metals are cut, the surface is shiny, but it quickly turns dull as an oxide layer forms.

This oxide layer is actually useful: it covers the metal and stops more oxygen from reaching it. But because these metals are so reactive, they must be stored under oil so that no air can reach them at all.

Common misunderstanding Some students think "unreactive metal = safer to handle." In fact, very reactive metals like sodium can be dangerous, which is why we never heat them in the classroom.

3. Some metals barely react with oxygen at all

Metals such as gold do not react with oxygen. We describe gold as inert. Silver reacts only very slowly — if left uncleaned, it slowly turns black as silver oxide forms.

The less reactive a metal is, the slower (or less likely) it reacts with oxygen.

4. Rusting — a special reaction of iron

When iron is left in damp air, it reacts with oxygen to form an orange-brown solid called iron oxide, better known as rust.

iron + oxygen → iron oxide

Rusting is important to know about because it is not useful — it changes iron so that it no longer has its strong, useful properties. A rusted iron girder can weaken so much that a structure collapses.

Key fact — remember this carefully Rusting only happens when both water and oxygen are present. If either one is missing, the iron will not rust — even though the reaction takes a long time either way, because iron is not very reactive.

5. Protecting iron from rusting

Because rusting is a problem, people protect iron in different ways:

  • Painting — a layer of paint stops oxygen in the air from reaching the iron.
  • Galvanising — covering iron with a layer of zinc, which also keeps oxygen away from the iron.

Both methods work using the same idea: if oxygen (or water) cannot reach the iron, the iron cannot rust.

Scientific Thinking

1. Interpreting evidence

Look at the four test tubes in Diagram 2. Tube 1 (moist air, open) went rusty. Tube 3 (dry air, stoppered) did not rust at all.

Your task: Using evidence from these two tubes only, explain what this tells you about the conditions needed for rusting.

2. Comparing observations

A student heats magnesium and iron separately in a Bunsen flame. The magnesium burns brightly and quickly. The iron glows slowly and does not burn brightly.

Your task: Which metal is more reactive with oxygen? Justify your answer using the observations given.

3. Making a prediction

Copper is less reactive than iron, but more reactive than gold.

Your task: Predict what would happen if a piece of copper were left in damp air for many months. Explain your reasoning.

Real World Science

Shipwrecks and old iron structures

Situation: An old iron ship left on the coast turns orange-brown and eventually falls apart.

Science connection: Sea air is often damp, giving both the water and oxygen iron needs to rust.

Why it matters: Engineers must plan for rusting when building anything from iron near water.

Painted gates and galvanised fences

Situation: Iron gates are often painted, and iron fences are often coated in zinc (galvanised).

Science connection: Both coatings stop oxygen and water from reaching the iron underneath.

Why it matters: This keeps structures safe and strong for much longer.

Storing reactive metals safely

Situation: In science labs, sodium, potassium and calcium are stored under oil.

Science connection: The oil stops oxygen and moisture in the air from reaching these very reactive metals.

Why it matters: Without the oil, these metals could react dangerously with air or moisture.

Exam Intelligence

These skills appear in the available Cambridge assessment material for this topic.

Writing word equations

You may be asked to write a word equation for a metal reacting with oxygen (for example, magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide). Always write the metal oxide name correctly — do not write "rust" as a chemical name.

Explaining the word "inert"

You may be asked to explain why a metal such as gold is described as inert. A strong answer explains that the metal does not react with oxygen (or other substances).

Identifying variables in a rusting investigation

Assessment questions often ask you to identify the variable being changed, a variable that must be controlled, or a conclusion based on a table of rusting results. Read the table carefully and quote the data in your answer.

Using a reactivity list

You may be given a list or chart of metals in order of reactivity and asked to predict how a metal will behave, based on its position in the list.
