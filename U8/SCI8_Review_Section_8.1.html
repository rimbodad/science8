Section Roadmap

What this lesson is about, and why it matters

In this section, you will learn about chemical reactions that give out energy. You already know that burning changes carbon and oxygen into carbon dioxide. In this lesson, you will find out why burning — and several other reactions — release energy, and how scientists compare how much energy different reactions release.

Why it matters Reactions that release energy are everywhere: cooking, car engines, hand warmers, rockets and even your own body all rely on exothermic reactions. Understanding them helps you explain temperature changes you observe in the lab and in daily life.

By the end of this lesson, I can...

  • Explain what an exothermic reaction is and describe the energy change that happens.
  • Describe what is needed for combustion (burning) to take place.
  • Recognise oxidation reactions and describe reactions of metals with oxygen, water and acid.
  • Interpret temperature data from an investigation and compare results fairly.
  • Identify variables in an investigation and explain how to make results reliable.

Core Concepts

Understand the main ideas, one step at a time

1. What does combustion need?

Main idea: Combustion (burning) needs three things at the same time: a fuel, oxygen, and enough heat to start the reaction.

Explanation: If any one of these three things is missing, burning cannot happen or will stop. This is why blowing out a candle removes heat, and why putting a lid on a pan fire cuts off the oxygen.

Example: Carbon burning in air: carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide.

Common misunderstandingSome students think burning only needs a match. A lit match provides the starting heat, but the fuel and oxygen must already be present for burning to continue.

2. What happens in an exothermic reaction?

Main idea: In an exothermic reaction, stored chemical energy in the reactants changes into thermal, light and sound energy, which dissipates into the surroundings.

Explanation: As atoms rearrange to form new substances, energy is released rather than absorbed. This is why the temperature of the surroundings rises during the reaction.

Example: When magnesium ribbon burns, chemical energy changes into thermal and light energy — you can feel the heat and see the bright light.

3. Oxidation reactions

Main idea: Whenever a substance combines with oxygen, an oxide is formed. This is called an oxidation reaction, and it is exothermic.

Explanation: The name of the oxide usually matches the metal: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.

Example: Burning magnesium ribbon in air produces magnesium oxide and releases heat and light.

4. Exothermic reactions with water and with acid

Main idea: Some metals react exothermically with water, and many metals react exothermically with acids.

Explanation: In both cases, the bonds in the reactants break and the atoms rearrange into new products. Hydrogen gas is usually one of the products.

Example: potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen.
magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen.

5. Comparing energy released fairly

Main idea: To compare how much energy two reactions release, scientists compare the rise in temperature (end temperature minus start temperature), not just the final temperature.

Explanation: If two reactions start at different temperatures, the one with the higher final temperature is not necessarily the one that released more energy.

Common misunderstandingThinking that a higher final temperature always means more energy was released. Always check the increase in temperature, since starting temperatures can differ.

Diagram Mastery

Use these diagrams to understand combustion and investigations visually

The Fire Triangle

What it shows: The three things needed for combustion — fuel, oxygen and heat — placed on the sides of a triangle.

What to notice: If one side of the triangle is "removed" (missing), the fire in the middle cannot exist.

Common mistake: Forgetting that oxygen must be present — thinking a fuel will burn in any gas.

Word Equation Model: Carbon + Oxygen

What it shows: One carbon atom joins with two oxygen atoms to make one particle of carbon dioxide.

What to notice: The same atoms are present before and after the reaction — they are simply rearranged.

Common mistake: Thinking that atoms are destroyed during burning. In fact, mass and atoms are conserved.

Investigation Apparatus: Measuring Temperature Rise

What it shows: A thermometer placed in a test tube of dilute acid, ready to measure temperature before and after adding magnesium ribbon.

What to notice: The thermometer bulb must stay in the liquid so it reads the temperature of the reacting mixture, not the air.

Common mistake: Reading the start temperature and the end temperature using different parts of the thermometer scale, or lifting the bulb out of the liquid.

Scientific Thinking

Think like a scientist about evidence and fair testing

Interpreting Evidence: Sofia and Marcus

Sofia measured 10 cm³ of hydrochloric acid, starting at 18 °C, and it reached 42 °C after adding magnesium. Marcus did the same experiment, starting at 21 °C and reaching 45 °C.

Marcus thought his reaction released more energy because his end temperature was higher. Sofia thought both reactions released the same amount of energy.

Think: Calculate the temperature rise for each experiment. What does comparing the rise (rather than the final value) tell you about whose reasoning is stronger?

Comparing Observations: Metal X and Metal Y

Zara tested metal X with hydrochloric acid; Arun tested metal Y with hydrochloric acid. Both used 10 cm³ of acid and saw bubbles of gas.

Think: What is the one variable Zara and Arun deliberately changed? What variables must stay the same for their comparison to be a fair test? If you noticed one description said a "small" piece of metal while the other did not specify a mass, what problem could that cause for their conclusion?

Choosing How to Present Data

Amal tested four different metals (A, B, C, D) with hydrochloric acid and recorded the rise in temperature for each.

Think: Is "type of metal" a continuous variable (like time or temperature) or a discrete/category variable? Which type of graph — bar chart or line graph — suits data with discrete categories, and why?

Real World Science

Where does this science appear in everyday life?

🧤 Chemical Hand Warmers

Situation: Small pouches used in cold weather get warm when shaken or opened.
Scientific connection: Iron powder inside reacts with oxygen — an oxidation reaction — releasing thermal energy.
Why it matters: This shows how the same reaction you studied (a metal combining with oxygen) can be used to keep people warm.

🚀 Hydrogen-Powered Rockets

Situation: Model rockets and real spacecraft can use hydrogen fuel.
Scientific connection: Hydrogen combines with oxygen in an exothermic combustion reaction, releasing a large amount of energy very quickly.
Why it matters: The rapid release of thermal, light and kinetic energy is what pushes the rocket forward.

🎆 Fireworks

Situation: Fireworks explode with bright light, loud sound and heat.
Scientific connection: Fuel inside the firework combusts rapidly, an exothermic reaction that releases chemical energy as light, sound and thermal energy almost instantly.
Why it matters: It shows that exothermic reactions can release several types of energy at once, not just heat.

Exercise 8.1A — Investigating an Exothermic Reaction

Learning objective: Identify variables and interpret results from an investigation into how the length of magnesium ribbon affects temperature rise.

Hint 1: Ask yourself — what is Sofia and Marcus deliberately changing between test tubes? That is your independent variable.

Hint 2: Ask yourself — what has to stay exactly the same in every test tube for the comparison to be fair (for example, the acid used)?

Thinking tip: When results seem to show "very little difference," think carefully about whether a wider range of values might reveal a clearer pattern.

Exercise 8.1B — Investigating Exothermic Reactions Between Metals and Acid

Learning objective: Evaluate whether an investigation comparing metal X and metal Y was set up as a fair test.

Hint 1: Look closely at how each metal sample is described. Are both amounts described in exactly the same way?

Hint 2: If one variable was not properly controlled, think about what that means for how reliable the conclusion is.

Thinking tip: A fair conclusion can only be drawn when every variable except the one being investigated is controlled.

Exam Intelligence

Skills seen in Cambridge assessment materials for this topic

📊 Completing Temperature-Change Tables

Progression Test questions have asked students to complete a table by calculating the temperature change (end temperature − start temperature). Always show your subtraction clearly and include the unit, °C.

🔤 Command Word: "Explain why..."

This command word appears when you must give a reason using scientific vocabulary, not just describe. For example: "Explain why the same volume of acid is used each time" expects an answer about fair testing and control variables.

⚖️ Deciding Endothermic or Exothermic from Data

Some questions give a start and end temperature and ask you to circle "endothermic" or "exothermic" and explain your choice. Base your answer on whether the temperature increased (exothermic) or decreased (endothermic).

📏 Explaining Measurements Taken

Questions may ask what measurements you would take to find out which reaction releases the most heat. A strong answer mentions measuring temperature before and after the reaction for each substance tested.

Exam tipWhen asked to explain a control variable (such as using the same volume of acid), always link your answer to the idea of a fair test — otherwise it is difficult to gain full marks.
