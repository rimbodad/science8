Unit Big Picture

What is this Unit really about?

Unit 8 tells one connected scientific story: chemical reactions always involve a transfer of energy, and metals react with oxygen, water and acid in predictable ways that depend on how reactive they are.

You started by learning that reactions can release energy (exothermic) or absorb energy (endothermic), and that this energy change can be measured with a thermometer. You then applied this idea to a very important family of reactions — metals reacting with oxygen, water and dilute acid. In every case, you saw the same pattern: more reactive metals react faster and more vigorously, evidence from experiments can be used to rank metals in a reactivity series, and word equations describe how reactants rearrange into new products without atoms being created or destroyed.

Along the way, you practised the scientific enquiry skills that link the whole Unit together: identifying variables, planning fair tests, measuring temperature change correctly, and using evidence — not assumptions — to reach conclusions.

Unit Big Ideas

The ideas to remember long after this Unit ends

BIG IDEA 1

Reactions transfer energy

Every chemical reaction either gives out thermal energy to the surroundings (exothermic) or takes it in (endothermic). Temperature change is the evidence used to tell the two apart.

BIG IDEA 2

Reactivity is a predictable pattern

Metals differ in how strongly they react with oxygen, water and acid. This reactivity can be ranked using experimental evidence, and it predicts safe handling, storage and everyday uses.

BIG IDEA 3

Word equations show conservation

metal + oxygen → oxide; metal + water → hydroxide (or oxide) + hydrogen; metal + acid → salt + hydrogen. Atoms rearrange into new substances — they are never created or destroyed.

BIG IDEA 4

Reaction rate is not the same as amount of product

A fast, vigorous reaction shows high reactivity — it does not automatically mean more product has been made. These are two separate pieces of evidence.

BIG IDEA 5

Fair testing makes evidence trustworthy

To compare reactions fairly, only one variable (for example, the metal) should change. Everything else — acid type, concentration, volume, mass of metal — must be controlled.

BIG IDEA 6

Very reactive metals need special safety rules

Potassium, sodium and calcium react so strongly with oxygen, water and acid that they are stored under oil and are never tested with dilute acid in a school laboratory.

Unit Knowledge Architecture

How the ideas in this Unit connect

Exothermic/endothermic ideas (8.1–8.2) apply directly to the three metal-reaction families (8.3–8.5), which are all organised by one reactivity series.

The same reactivity order explains behaviour in every family of reaction studied in this Unit — this is why the reactivity series is the "spine" connecting Sections 8.3, 8.4 and 8.5.

Vocabulary Master List

The scientific language of Unit 8

Energy Change Vocabulary
exothermic
A reaction that gives out thermal energy — temperature rises.
endothermic reaction
A reaction that absorbs thermal energy and forms new substances — temperature falls.
endothermic process
A physical change (e.g. melting) that absorbs energy but forms no new substance.
combustion
Burning — a fuel reacts with oxygen and releases energy.
dissipate
To spread out into the surroundings, e.g. released thermal energy.
insulator
A material (e.g. polystyrene cup) that reduces thermal energy transfer, improving accuracy.
Reactions of Metals Vocabulary
oxidation
A reaction in which a substance combines with oxygen to form an oxide.
reactivity
How readily and quickly a metal reacts with oxygen, water or acid.
reactivity series
An evidence-based ranking of metals from most to least reactive.
inert
Does not react (or reacts extremely slowly) with other substances, e.g. gold.
rust
Iron oxide — forms only when iron reacts with BOTH oxygen and water.
metal hydroxide
Product formed, with hydrogen, when a metal reacts with water.
metal oxide
Product formed when a metal reacts with oxygen or with steam.
steam
Water in the gas state; its particles carry more energy than cold water particles.
salt
Compound formed when a metal replaces the hydrogen in an acid.
dilute
Mixed with a large amount of water, making a solution weaker.
hydrogen
Light gas produced when reactive metals react with water or acid; tested with a "squeaky pop".
word equation
A reaction shown using the names of reactants and products, e.g. metal + acid → salt + hydrogen.
Scientific Enquiry Vocabulary
fair test
An investigation where only one variable changes and all others are controlled.
independent variable
The one factor deliberately changed in an investigation.
dependent variable
The factor measured to see if it has changed.
control variable
A factor kept the same to ensure a fair, valid comparison.
reactant / product
Substances present before (reactant) and after (product) a reaction.
displacement of water
Method of collecting a gas by letting it push water out of an inverted, water-filled container.
Section Knowledge Anchors

Quick locators for each section

8.1 Exothermic Reactions

Central idea: Combustion, oxidation, and metal reactions with water/acid all release thermal energy to the surroundings.

Memorable takeaway: Always compare the temperature rise, not the final temperature, to judge which reaction released more energy.

Connects to: 8.4 and 8.5 — the same metal-water and metal-acid reactions are exothermic examples.

8.2 Endothermic Reactions

Central idea: Some reactions absorb thermal energy, so the surroundings cool down; this is different from an endothermic process (no new substance).

Memorable takeaway: If a new substance forms and temperature drops → endothermic reaction. If no new substance forms (e.g. ice melting) → endothermic process.

Connects to: 8.1 — the direct opposite case, used to strengthen the "is this a reaction?" thinking test.

8.3 Metals and their Reactions with Oxygen

Central idea: Metals react with oxygen (oxidation) at very different rates, and rusting needs both oxygen AND water.

Memorable takeaway: Painting and galvanising protect iron by blocking oxygen (and water) from reaching it.

Connects to: 8.4 and 8.5 — introduces the reactivity series used throughout the rest of the Unit.

8.4 Reactions of Metals with Water

Central idea: Metals react with cold water, hot water or steam at rates that match their position in the reactivity series.

Memorable takeaway: Steam particles carry more energy than cold water particles, so a metal that seems unreactive in cold water may react well with steam.

Connects to: 8.3 — same reactivity order; 8.5 — same experimental thinking (gas collection, safety).

8.5 Reactions of Metals with Dilute Acids

Central idea: metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen; reaction speed provides evidence for ranking metal reactivity.

Memorable takeaway: A fast reaction proves high reactivity — it does NOT automatically mean more product was made.

Connects to: 8.3 and 8.4 — completes the reactivity-series evidence built across the whole Unit.

Common Misconceptions — Merged Across the Unit

Ideas that often get muddled

❌ A faster or more vigorous reaction always makes more product. ✔ Reaction rate (speed) and the total amount of product are two different things. A fast reaction just means high reactivity — the total product depends on the quantities of reactants used.
❌ The highest final temperature shows which reaction gave out the most energy. ✔ You must compare the temperature rise (end − start), because two reactions can start at different temperatures.
❌ Metal "disappears" or is destroyed in a reaction. ✔ Atoms are conserved — the metal's atoms become part of the new product (oxide, hydroxide or salt); they are rearranged, never destroyed.
❌ If a metal doesn't react with water (or oxygen), it must be completely inert. ✔ "Does not react with water" only describes that one reaction. True inertness (like gold) means a metal barely reacts with almost anything — water, oxygen AND acid.
❌ Steam is a different chemical substance from water. ✔ Steam is water in the gas state — chemically identical to liquid water, but its particles have much more energy, so it reacts faster.
❌ Rusting only needs oxygen, or only needs water. ✔ Rusting needs BOTH oxygen and water present together. Removing either one prevents or greatly slows rusting.
❌ A temperature drop means "nothing is happening". ✔ A temperature drop is strong evidence that a change IS happening — it shows the change is endothermic.
Investigation and Enquiry Review

How your scientific enquiry skills grew across the Unit

Every investigation in this Unit trained the same core skills: identifying variables, controlling variables for a fair test, choosing the correct measurement, and using evidence rather than assumption to draw a conclusion.

Measuring energy change (8.1 / 8.2)

Skill focus: measuring temperature before and after a reaction, using an insulated container to reduce heat loss, and comparing the temperature change fairly across different trials.

Testing conditions for rusting (8.3)

Skill focus: setting up several test tubes with different combinations of air and water, keeping the type/size of nail the same (control variable), and concluding that both oxygen and water are necessary.

Comparing metal reactivity with water (8.4)

Skill focus: using sandpaper to clean a metal surface before testing, observing bubble production, and recognising when a reaction needs hotter conditions (steam) to become visible.

Ranking reactivity with acid (8.5)

Skill focus: identifying and controlling variables (acid type, concentration, volume, metal size/mass), timing gas collection by displacement of water, and safely excluding potassium, sodium and calcium from testing.

Transferable enquiry skill: In every investigation, only ONE variable should change at a time. If the type of acid, concentration, volume, or metal mass differs between test tubes, the comparison is not valid — no matter how careful the observations are.

Diagram and Model Mastery

The apparatus and models you must recognise

Combustion needs all three — fuel, oxygen and heat — together. Removing any one stops the fire.

Hydrogen gas is collected by pushing water out of an inverted, water-filled test tube — used in 8.4 and 8.5.

Comparing all four tubes gives evidence that rusting needs both oxygen AND water present together.

Scientific Reasoning Review

Thinking skills this Unit developed

Comparing evidence fairly

Comparing temperature rise, not final temperature; comparing reaction time using the same mass/size of metal.

Distinguishing reaction vs. process

Asking "has a new substance formed?" to decide if a change is a chemical reaction or a physical process.

Necessary vs. sufficient conditions

Rusting needs oxygen AND water — neither condition alone is sufficient.

Using rate as evidence for reactivity

Faster bubbling or shorter gas-collection time is used as evidence to rank metals — always checked against a fair test.

Evaluating investigations

Spotting when an investigation is NOT a fair test (e.g. different acid concentrations, unequal metal sizes) and explaining why the result cannot be trusted.

Predicting from a pattern

Using a metal's position in the reactivity series to predict how it will behave with oxygen, water or acid — even without testing it directly.

Real World Science

Where this Unit's science shows up in everyday life

Self-heating cans and cold packs

Self-heating cans use an exothermic reaction (calcium oxide + water). Instant cold packs use an endothermic process (a salt dissolving in water) to cool quickly.

Copper water pipes

Copper is used for pipes and roofing because it barely reacts with water — unlike more reactive metals such as magnesium.

Protecting iron structures

Painting and galvanising (coating with zinc) stop oxygen and water reaching iron, preventing rust from weakening bridges, cars and buildings.

Storing reactive metals safely

Potassium, sodium and calcium are stored under oil in labs and industry because they react readily with oxygen and moisture in the air.

Assessment Preparation

Getting ready for Cambridge-style questions

Word equations: Practise writing equations in the correct order — reactants on the left, products on the right, with a single arrow (→). Marks are usually given separately for correct reactants and correct products.
Command words: "Describe" asks what happens; "Explain" asks why it happens; "Compare" asks for similarities and differences; "Evaluate" asks you to judge how reliable or fair an investigation is.
Diagram interpretation: Be ready to read apparatus diagrams (gas collection, rusting test tubes, temperature apparatus) and explain what each part is doing.
Identifying unsafe metals: Be ready to name which metals (potassium, sodium, calcium) must never be tested directly with acid, and explain why using reactivity.
Evaluating fair tests: Look carefully for unequal volumes, concentrations, or metal sizes between test tubes — these are common exam scenarios (as seen in the Workbook exercises).
